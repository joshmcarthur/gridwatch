## Simple explanation

Electricity has to travel along wires. Each line can carry only so much. When a line is full, cheaper electricity on one side cannot all get to the other, and the wholesale prices come apart.

A wholesale price is what generators and retailers pay each other in a short slice of the day. A named place on the high-voltage grid is a node. Each node can have its own price. When the wires between two places can carry enough, those prices stay close. When they cannot, the prices separate.

That split is a transmission constraint showing up in the price. The price does not name the line.

## A more technical explanation

The market sets a wholesale price at every node so that supply and demand balance, without sending more power along a line than that line can take. If a line is at its limit, the next megawatt on the cheaper side cannot reach the more expensive side, and the two prices pull apart.

Three places are enough to watch that split: Ōtāhuhu in Auckland, Haywards near Wellington, and Benmore in the Waitaki. Haywards minus Benmore is the North Island / South Island spread. Ōtāhuhu minus Haywards is the step inside the North Island.

The Cook Strait cable is one path that can reach its limit. Other lines on each island can too. The spread says the prices came apart. It does not say which line was full, or how much spare capacity was left.

What a node is, and why Auckland, Wellington, and the South Island can differ in the same half-hour, is on [nodal pricing](/explainers/nodal-pricing/).

## Historical example

On Monday 27 July 2026, in the half-hour from 17:30 to 18:00, Haywards sat with Ōtāhuhu, not with Benmore. The Haywards–Benmore spread was $2,281.97 per megawatt-hour. Ōtāhuhu–Haywards was $442.61.

The split was between the islands, with a further step up into Auckland. The chart shows that shape. It does not name the line that was full.
