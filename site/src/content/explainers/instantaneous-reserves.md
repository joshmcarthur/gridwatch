## Simple explanation

The grid needs spare generating capacity sitting ready, in case a power station trips. That spare is instantaneous reserve. The market pays for it, so reserve has its own wholesale price, separate from the price of the electricity people use.

There are two kinds, and they match the two classes in the Electricity Authority's reserve price files: fast reserve must act within 6 seconds, and slow reserve within 60 seconds. A high reserve price means that reserve was expensive in that half-hour. It does not mean the grid was unsafe.

## A more technical explanation

A plant that trips takes its megawatts off the system at once. Instantaneous reserve is the capacity that can replace them in seconds, so frequency does not fall too far. Frequency keeping is a different product, which trims generation and load from moment to moment to hold 50 Hz. [Frequency keeping](/explainers/frequency-keeping/) describes how this works.

The market clears energy and reserve together. A megawatt held as reserve cannot also be sold as energy in the same interval, so a tight evening can lift both prices.

Each island has its own fast and slow reserve prices. Like the wholesale price, they are in dollars per megawatt-hour ($/MWh), and a megawatt-hour is 1,000 kilowatt-hours.

## Historical example

On Monday 27 July 2026, in the half-hour from 5:30pm to 6:00pm, the North Island slow reserve price was $2,154.45 per megawatt-hour and fast reserve was $49.93. Both South Island reserve prices were $0.

So reserve was expensive in the North Island that evening and cost nothing in the South Island.
