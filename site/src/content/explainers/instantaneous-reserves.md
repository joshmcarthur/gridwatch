## Simple explanation

The grid needs spare generating capacity sitting ready, in case a power station trips. That spare is instantaneous reserve. The market pays for it, so reserve has its own wholesale price, separate from the price of the electricity people use.

There are two kinds. Fast reserve must act within 6 seconds. Slow reserve must act within 60 seconds. A high reserve price means that backup was dear in that half-hour. It does not mean the grid was unsafe.

## A more technical explanation

A plant that trips takes its megawatts off the system at once. Instantaneous reserve is the capacity that can replace them in seconds, so frequency does not fall too far. Frequency keeping is a different product: it trims generation and load from moment to moment to hold 50 Hz. [Frequency keeping](/explainers/frequency-keeping/) covers that service.

The market clears energy and reserve together. A megawatt held as reserve cannot also be sold as energy in the same interval, so a tight evening can lift both prices.

This site publishes reserve prices for each island, for fast reserve and slow reserve. The daily page shows the day's lowest, average, and highest, and a chart through the day when the files support it. The files do not name which plant was held as reserve, or which trip the market was covering.

A wholesale price is in dollars per megawatt-hour ($/MWh). A megawatt-hour is 1,000 kilowatt-hours.

## Historical example

On Monday 27 July 2026, in the half-hour from 17:30 to 18:00, the North Island slow reserve price was $2,154.45 per megawatt-hour. Fast reserve was $49.93. Both South Island reserve prices were $0.

Backup was dear in the North Island that evening, and cheap in the South Island. That is a price for spare capacity, not a reading of how safe the grid was.
