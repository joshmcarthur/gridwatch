---
title: Hydro scarcity
description: What it means when the lakes run low, and why that is a different story from one expensive evening.
---

## Simple explanation

New Zealand gets a lot of its electricity from hydro lakes. Rain and snow fill the lakes. Stations then run water through turbines. When inflows stay low for months, the lakes run down. Other stations have to make up the difference, and wholesale prices tend to rise.

That long shortage of water is hydro scarcity. A dry year is the same idea stretched across a year. One expensive evening, on its own, is not a dry year.

## A more technical explanation

Hydro is stored energy. Operators choose when to run water today against keeping it for later. When storage is high, hydro can cover a lot of the load and keep dearer plants off. When storage is low, those dearer plants run more, and the wholesale price tends to sit higher for weeks, not for one half-hour.

A wholesale price is what generators and retailers pay each other in a short slice of the day. A spike can still happen when the lakes are full: a still evening, a full Cook Strait link, or tight reserve can lift the price while there is plenty of water in the catchments.

This site publishes how much hydro generated on a day. It has no lake level, no inflow series, and no water-value. So it can show hydro's share of the mix, and it cannot show whether the lakes were scarce. [Wind and wholesale prices](/explainers/wind/) covers another fuel that moves with the weather. [What makes a generator marginal](/explainers/marginal-generator/) covers how a dearer plant can set the price when hydro is held back or already fully used.
