## Simple explanation

Wind farms make electricity when the wind blows. On a still day they make less, so other stations have to make up the difference. Those other stations often cost more to run, and the wholesale price can rise.

A wholesale price is what generators and retailers pay each other in a short slice of the day. Wind itself has no fuel cost, but it does not run on demand. The market still has to find the megawatts somewhere else when the wind drops.

## A more technical explanation

Wind output moves with the weather, half-hour to half-hour. Hydro can often ramp to cover a gap. Gas plants and the Huntly units that burn coal or gas can ramp too, and they tend to cost more.

What ran is not the same as a wind forecast, and it does not name which plant set the wholesale price. Low wind on one evening is one reason other fuels ran harder. It is not, by itself, the whole story of a price spike. The last megawatt taken is on [what makes a generator marginal](/explainers/marginal-generator/).

## Historical example

Monday 27 July 2026 was a low-wind weekday. Wind averaged 186.5 megawatts across the day, in a range of 91.1 to 289.3 megawatts. In the half-hour from 17:30 to 18:00, national wind was 150.0 megawatts and North Island wind was 69.3 megawatts.

Gas plants, the coal-or-gas units, and North Island hydro supplied the evening rise. Wind did not.
