## Simple explanation

New Zealand's wholesale electricity market does not have one price for the whole country. A wholesale price is what generators and retailers pay each other in a short slice of the day, not the rate on a household bill.

Each named place on the high-voltage grid has its own wholesale price. That place is a node, also called a point of connection. Nodal pricing means the price at a node is the cost of supplying a little more electricity there, given the plants that can reach it and how much the wires between them can carry.

So Auckland, Wellington, and the South Island can have different wholesale prices in the same half-hour. When the Cook Strait cable and the rest of the grid can carry enough, those prices stay close, and when they cannot, the prices come apart.

## A more technical explanation

Power stations offer electricity in bulk. The market then sets a wholesale price at every node so that supply and demand balance, without sending more power along a line than that line can take. If a line is full, cheaper electricity on one side cannot all get to the other, and the two prices separate.

Prices are set for each half-hour of the day, in dollars per megawatt-hour ($/MWh). A megawatt-hour is 1,000 kilowatt-hours, the same kWh unit as the line on a power bill.

Three nodes are enough to watch the two islands:

- Ōtāhuhu in Auckland, for the upper North Island
- Haywards near Wellington, the North Island end of the Cook Strait cable
- Benmore in the Waitaki, the South Island end of that cable

The cable is a high-voltage direct current (HVDC) link. It joins the islands but does not make them one market all the time. If it is full, or one island is short of power, the wholesale price on one side can pull away from the other.

Haywards minus Benmore is the North Island / South Island spread. A spread is one node's price minus the other's in the same half-hour, so a positive Haywards–Benmore spread means the North Island is more expensive. Ōtāhuhu minus Haywards is the step inside the North Island. Ōtāhuhu minus Benmore is the other two added together, so it is in the numbers table rather than drawn as its own line.

The files behind the chart are dispatch energy prices: the published wholesale price at each node. They can still be revised after the day, and they do not forecast tomorrow.

The chart does not name which line was full, which plant set the price, or whether a household plan passed a spike through.

## Historical example

Monday 27 July 2026 was an ordinary winter weekday. In the half-hour from 5:30pm to 6:00pm, the wholesale price was $3,296.04 per megawatt-hour at Ōtāhuhu, $2,853.43 at Haywards and $571.46 at Benmore.

Haywards sat with Ōtāhuhu, not with Benmore. The Haywards–Benmore spread was $2,281.97 and Ōtāhuhu–Haywards was $442.61, so Ōtāhuhu minus Benmore came to $2,724.58. The split was between the islands, with a further step up into Auckland.

The spike lasted four half-hours, from 5:30pm to 7:30pm, and then prices dropped. Across the whole day the averages were much lower: Ōtāhuhu $248.67, Haywards $214.72, Benmore $70.60.
